Moving to Panama City: a guide from people who did it.

We're LifeBridge, an English-speaking church in Panama City. Our congregation comes from more than twenty countries and nearly all of us arrived here from somewhere else.

First weeks Neighborhoods Getting around Daily life Meeting people
Arriving

The first few weeks.

Sort out a local phone number first. Almost everything in Panama runs through WhatsApp — your landlord, your child's school, the plumber, the man who fixes your air conditioning.

Panama uses the US dollar, so there's no new currency to learn. There is, however, paperwork. Opening a bank account or signing a lease takes longer than you expect, and patience helps more than urgency.

Addresses work differently here. Places are found by building name, neighborhood and landmark rather than by street number, and taxi drivers will ask you which building.

Visa and residency rules change often enough that anything we wrote would go stale. Check the official government sources, or talk to an immigration lawyer.

Where to live

The neighborhoods, and how they actually feel.

The city is a set of neighborhoods with genuinely different characters, and which one suits you has more to do with how you want to spend a Tuesday evening than with anything on a map.

Marbella and Obarrio are the business heart — towers, restaurants, and enough on foot that you can live without driving every day. Bella Vista and El Cangrejo are older and more mixed.

Spend an evening in a neighborhood before you commit to a year in it. The difference between them is obvious in person and invisible on a map.

Transport

Getting around the city.

The metro is clean, cheap and genuinely useful if you live and work near it. Look at where the lines run before you sign a lease — it changes your daily life more than almost anything else.

Ride-hailing apps work well and most newcomers use them by default. Street taxis are cheaper, but you agree the fare before you get in.

Driving is workable and traffic is not. Rush hour is genuinely bad, rain makes it worse, and plenty of people here don't own a car at all.

Daily life

What daily life is actually like.

WhatsApp runs the country. Businesses, schools, building administrators and friends all reach you there. If you're used to email and phone calls, this is the single biggest adjustment.

The pace is slower than many newcomers expect, and treating that as rudeness is the fastest way to have a bad year. Appointments drift. Relationships come before transactions.

Groceries take some relearning. The big supermarkets carry most of what you're used to, some of it imported and priced accordingly. The fresh markets are better and cheaper.

You can get by in Panama City with English, and you'll have a much better time if you don't. Even clumsy Spanish changes how people respond to you.

The hard part

The part nobody warns you about.

The logistics of moving here are solvable. You'll find an apartment, you'll learn the roads, you'll work out where to buy chicken. What nobody warns you about is the quiet.

This is the part that takes longest and the part most people are least prepared for. Adults don't make friends by accident; you have to keep turning up somewhere until the same faces become familiar.

There are more of those somewheres here than you'd think. The expat Facebook and WhatsApp groups are active and full of people in exactly your position.

Pick something and go back to it every week, even when you don't feel like it. The going back is what makes the difference, not the choosing.

We should be straight with you: this guide was written by a church.

LifeBridge is an English-speaking church that meets in the World Trade Center on Sunday mornings. We wrote this because it's the guide we needed — our congregation is from more than twenty countries and most of us arrived not knowing a single person. If church is something you're looking for, we'd be glad to see you. If it isn't, we hope the rest of this was useful anyway.
